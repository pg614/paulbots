Speed to Lead: How Fast to Answer a New Inquiry (Without Being On Call)

Answer a new inquiry as fast as you reasonably can, ideally within the hour and sooner when you can manage it. The best-known research on this found that businesses that tried to reach a lead within an hour were far more likely to have a real conversation than businesses that waited even one more hour. You don't need to be glued to your phone to get there. You need a simple system that replies right away, collects the basics, and gets the right person involved.

This guide is for small-business owners, especially local service businesses around Columbus, Ohio, who get leads from their website, phone, Google Business Profile, or ads and know some of them slip away. It covers what "speed to lead" means, what the research actually says, a step-by-step response workflow, the consent rules to respect, and where an AI helper fits.

What Speed to Lead Means

Speed to lead is the time from the moment someone reaches out to the moment they get a real response from your business. "Real" matters. An automatic "we got your message" email with no next step isn't much of a response. A reply that confirms what they asked for, answers a basic question, and tells them what happens next is.

A lead here means anyone who raised their hand: a website form, a chat on your site, a missed call, a Google Business Profile message, or a reply to an ad. These people already took a step toward you. That's different from cold prospecting, where you reach out to strangers.

Why the first reply carries so much weight

When a homeowner's furnace quits or a family needs a dentist this week, they often contact more than one business. The first business that responds clearly gets the first real conversation. That doesn't guarantee the job, but it's hard to win a job you never get to discuss.

What the Research Says About Lead Response Time

The most cited work on this topic is "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads," published in Harvard Business Review in March 2011 by James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington. Two findings are worth knowing:

  • Most businesses were slow. The authors sent a test web inquiry to 2,241 US companies. 37% responded within an hour, 16% within one to 24 hours, 24% took more than 24 hours, and 23% never responded. Among companies that did respond within 30 days, the average response time was 42 hours.
  • Leads cooled quickly. In a separate analysis of 1.25 million leads at 42 US companies, firms that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly 7 times as likely to qualify it (have a meaningful conversation with a decision maker) as firms that tried an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or longer.

Reading the research honestly

This research came from companies with sales teams in industries like financial services, software, and education, not from local plumbers or salons. It's also more than a decade old. So don't treat "7 times" as a promise for your business. Treat it as strong evidence of a direction: interest fades fast, and the businesses that answer first get more conversations.

Why Slow Replies Happen in Small Businesses

Most owners aren't slow because they don't care. They're slow because the work is in the field, not at a desk.

  • You're on a job. A plumber under a sink or a hygienist with a patient can't answer a form submission.
  • Leads land in too many places. Website forms go to one inbox, Facebook messages to another, voicemails to a phone.
  • Nobody owns the first reply. Everyone assumes someone else answered.
  • After-hours inquiries wait until morning. An evening request can sit 12 hours or more. (We covered this in our guide to after-hours calls.)
  • The first reply feels like it has to be perfect. So it waits until someone has time to write a full quote.

The HBR authors pointed to similar causes in larger companies, such as checking leads in a daily batch instead of as they arrive, and routing rules that slowed things down.

A Simple Speed-to-Lead Workflow

You don't need a big sales system. You need a few steps that happen the same way every time.

Step 1: Put every lead in one place

List every way people contact you: website form, website chat, phone, text, Google Business Profile, email, and ads. Decide where each one lands and make sure all of them notify the same person or shared inbox.

Step 2: Send an instant, useful first reply

Within moments, the lead should hear back with three things:

  1. Confirmation of what they asked for ("Thanks, Dana, we got your request about a water heater").
  2. One or two simple questions that help you prepare (address or ZIP, urgency, best time).
  3. A clear next step ("Someone from our team will call you shortly. If you'd rather, you can pick a time here.").

This is the part an automated helper handles well, because it doesn't depend on someone being free.

Step 3: Hand off to a person fast

The automated reply buys you time, but it doesn't replace you. Set a target, such as a human reply within the hour during business hours, and decide who covers it. For urgent requests, define what "urgent" means and how the alert reaches someone.

Step 4: Follow up if they go quiet

Many leads don't answer the first time. Plan a short, polite sequence, such as a check-in later that day and another the next day, then stop. Every message should make it easy to say "not interested" or "stop."

Step 5: Record the outcome

Mark each lead as booked, quoted, not a fit, or no response. Without this step you can't tell whether faster replies are helping.

Choosing the Right First-Response Channel

The best channel is usually the one the lead used. Here's how they compare:

A practical mix for many local businesses: a website helper that captures the inquiry, an instant text or email confirmation where the lead gave permission, and a person who calls back.

US Rules to Respect: Consent, Opt-Outs, and AI Voices

Speed should never come at the cost of trust. This is a plain-English overview, not legal advice. Talk to an attorney about your specific situation.

The TCPA and automated calls and texts

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) limits calls and texts made with automated systems or artificial or prerecorded voices. In February 2024, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) confirmed that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" under the TCPA, so calls using them generally need the person's prior express consent unless an exemption applies.

What that means for you: If someone asks you to contact them, get clear permission on your form ("I agree you may contact me by phone, email, or text about my request") and keep a record of it.

Opt-outs must be easy and fast

Under FCC rules that took effect April 11, 2025, people can revoke consent in any reasonable way. Replying "stop," "quit," "end," "revoke," "opt out," "cancel," or "unsubscribe" to a text counts. You must honor opt-out requests within a reasonable time, no later than 10 business days, and you can't force people to use only one opt-out method. Parts of these rules have had separate effective dates, so check the current status with your attorney or messaging provider.

Email follow-up and CAN-SPAM

Commercial emails must not use misleading headers or subject lines, must include your physical postal address, and must offer a clear way to opt out, which you honor within 10 business days. The FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide covers the details.

Keep it honest

If an AI helper answers, don't have it pretend to be a specific human. Let it say it's an automated assistant, answer only from information you approve, and hand off to a person when it's unsure.

Common Problems and How to Fix Them

"We reply fast, but the reply doesn't help."

A generic "thanks, we'll be in touch" doesn't move anything. Add one helpful question and a concrete next step.

"The bot answers things it shouldn't."

Limit what it can say. Give it approved answers for hours, service area, and services, and have it pass pricing questions, complaints, and anything unusual to a person.

"We get alerts, but nobody acts on them."

Name one owner for each shift and a backup. An alert nobody owns is just noise.

"Leads get too many messages."

Cap your follow-ups and stop the moment someone replies, books, or opts out.

"Our forms don't collect permission."

Add a clear consent line to every form and keep the record. Without it, hold off on automated texts and calls.

How to Measure Whether It's Working

Pick a few simple numbers and check them monthly:

  • Median time to first reply: from inquiry to the first useful response.
  • Median time to human contact: from inquiry to a real person reaching them.
  • Contact rate: the share of leads you actually spoke or chatted with.
  • Booked rate: the share of leads that became an appointment or quote.
  • No-response rate: leads that never got a reply. Aim for zero.
  • Opt-out rate: if it climbs, your follow-up is too pushy.

Measure for a month before making changes, then compare a month after. Your own numbers will tell you more than any industry average.

Our Perspective

Speed matters, but speed alone isn't the goal. The goal is that no one who reaches out to your business is left wondering whether you got their message. Automate the first reply and the reminders, and keep people in charge of the relationship. A fast, honest "we've got you, here's what happens next" followed by a real person beats a flood of automated messages every time.

FAQ

How fast should a small business respond to a new lead?

As fast as you reasonably can, ideally within an hour. Research published in Harvard Business Review found firms that tried to contact leads within an hour were nearly 7 times as likely to qualify them as firms that tried an hour later. An instant automated confirmation plus a person following up soon after is a realistic target for most small businesses.

What is a good lead response time?

There's no single standard for every industry. Start by measuring your current median response time, then aim to give every lead a useful reply within the hour during business hours, and an instant confirmation after hours.

Can AI respond to leads for my business?

Yes. An AI helper can greet a website visitor, answer from information you approve, collect contact details, and pass the lead to your team. Keep a person involved for quotes, judgment calls, and sensitive topics.

Do I need permission to text a new lead?

For automated texts, generally yes. Collect clear consent on your forms, keep a record, and honor "STOP" and other opt-out requests quickly. Check with an attorney for your specific situation.

Is speed to lead the same as lead reactivation?

No. Speed to lead is about answering new inquiries fast. Lead reactivation is about reconnecting, with permission, with people who inquired in the past and didn't buy.
